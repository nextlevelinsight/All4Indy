## 2. Fee landscape and "highly competitive" take rates

The market has three tiers.

- **Retailer marketplaces (Amazon, Kobo, Apple, Google):** the author keeps 70% where conditions are met. Amazon drops to 35% outside its conditions and deducts delivery costs. Apple and Google are at 70% flat. Kobo is 70% from $2.99.
- **Aggregators (D2D):** about 10% commission on top of the store's cut, so authors net about 60% of retail. Bookshop's ebook split shows the same shape: 60% to the author, 30% to the bookstore, about 10% to D2D.
- **Direct-sales tools:** 5% to 10% plus processing. Payhip is 5% on the free plan and 0% at $99/month. Gumroad is 10% + $0.50 direct and 30% on its own marketplace. Lemon Squeezy is 5% + $0.50 as merchant of record. Real all-in cost is roughly 8% to 12% on a $5 to $10 ebook once Stripe or PayPal fees are added (my estimate from the listed rates, not a sourced figure).

**Sell-through take rate that looks highly competitive (my judgment, not sourced):**

- The anchor is 70% to the author, which is what Amazon, Apple, Google and Kobo advertise. A 30% platform take is therefore par, not a differentiator.
- Reaching "highly competitive" means the author nets 80% to 85% after processing, i.e. a platform take of about 12% to 15% all-in. That beats Gumroad's 10% + $0.50 once processing is added, and it is far below Gumroad's 30% Discover fee, which is the closest analogue to a curated marketplace where the platform finds the buyer.
- A split rate is defensible: lower (about 10% to 15%) for sales the author drives, higher (about 25% to 30%) for sales the marketplace originates. That matches the market precedent of Gumroad (10% vs 30%) and Bookshop.
- Reasoning: merchant-of-record services already cost 5% plus a flat fee, so any platform adding discovery and marketing must show it earns the rest of the margin. Taking 30% on all sales would only match the retailers and give authors no reason to switch.

**Link-out referral model (my judgment):**

- Bookshop's affiliate rate is 10% of list price to authors and 10% to bookstores. Libro.fm and Amazon Associates style programs exist, but their terms were not verified here.
- For a platform paid by authors' own sites, 5% to 10% of the sale looks reasonable, and a flat per-click or per-sale fee below the author's net margin would feel fairer. Print margins are thin (D2D: about 45% of list minus print cost), so 10% of list could be a large share of the author's profit. A low single-digit percent or a capped fee is safer for physical books.
- Only charge for referrals the platform can attribute. Bookshop's attribution window is 48 hours, which shows how contested attribution is.

## 3. Gaps and opportunities, and where The Open Shelf is NOT differentiated

**Possible gaps (inferences from the research):**

1. **Opt-in buyer email at checkout.** Retailers do not share buyer emails (Amazon: not found, likely no (U)). Direct tools like BookFunnel and Payhip do give email tools, but not a shared marketplace with consented sharing. A marketplace that gets the reader's explicit opt-in could be unique. No competitor page I found described this.
2. **Curated discovery with indie-friendly splits.** Gumroad's marketplace charges 30%. Retailers' discovery is dominated by Amazon. A human-curated storefront is a plausible niche, but nothing I found proves demand.
3. **Transparent, near-real-time reporting.** KDP and Kobo pay on roughly 45-60 day cycles. Payhip pays immediately after a sale. A portal that shows sales as they happen, with a clear fee breakdown, is possible but unproven as a purchase driver.
4. **Quality filtering.** Amazon relies on disclosure and a 3-per-day cap. D2D is stricter. A curated, human-reviewed marketplace is a possible differentiator if enforced.
5. **Hybrid sell-through plus link-out in one portal.** I did not find a platform that does both with unified reporting. Bookshop and D2D are now joined for ebooks, but they cover a bookstore audience.

**Where it is not differentiated:**

- **DRM-free:** Amazon now allows DRM-free EPUB/PDF downloads (Jan 2026), Bookshop encourages DRM-free, and many small stores exist. Libreture lists 543 DRM-free shops (U, niche directory).
- **Lifetime ownership of files:** this follows from DRM-free downloads. It is a message, not a moat.
- **Merchant-of-record tax handling:** Lemon Squeezy, Gumroad and Payhip already do it.
- **Physical books:** Bookshop owns the independent-store narrative; Amazon and D2D handle print on demand.
- **Audiobooks:** Libro.fm, Voices by INaudio, Spotify and Google already exist. Competing here needs a partner.

## 4. Five biggest threats / reasons similar attempts failed

1. **Amazon sets reader habits and moves toward the same promise.** DRM-free downloads from KDP titles went live in Jan 2026, narrowing the "you own it" pitch.
2. **Aggregators and bookstores are converging on the indie ebook space.** The D2D-Bookshop partnership (Feb 2026) puts DRM-free indie ebooks in front of Bookshop's audience with an existing author payout of 60% (per Kris Austin). The Open Shelf would have to beat that.
3. **Cold-start supply and demand.** Marketplaces need readers and authors at once. Pronoun (Macmillan, free distribution with no commission) shut in 2017 with little explanation. Entitle, an ebook subscription, closed in 2015 after struggling to attract consumers (via search summaries; verify before citing).
4. **Thin margins and rising fixed fees.** Even established players are raising fees: D2D added activation and annual fees in April 2026. Bookshop's ebooks route 30% to stores. Low take rates may not fund curation and marketing. Apple's in-app 30% rule already forced Bookshop to make readers buy on its website before using the app (PW, 2025).
5. **Trust and compliance risk.** Authors must trust a new payer. Merchant-of-record duties (tax, refunds, chargebacks, piracy of DRM-free files) fall on the platform. Gumroad reportedly raised payout minimums to $100 (U), which shows how payout friction creeps in. AI slop pressure also forces policy work: Amazon added disclosure rules and daily caps.
